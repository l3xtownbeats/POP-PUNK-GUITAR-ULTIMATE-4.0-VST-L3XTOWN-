## 2. Genre-wide performance model supplied

- Meter: predominantly 4/4.
- Tempo references: classic 2000s pop-punk commonly 150–190 BPM, with some examples above 200 BPM; modern 2020-era references around 85–130 BPM. These are broad stylistic ranges, not hard constraints.
- Common rhythmic foundation: steady eighth-note guitar patterns; frequent downpicking is a defining performance colour in many classic fast passages, but not a universal rule.
- Power chords commonly use two or three strings, with unused strings muted; full barre chords are not always used in riff-based parts.
- Bass may reinforce guitar riffs at the octave or in unison; drum patterns often establish a straight punk backbeat.
- Classic arrangements may favour straight drive; modern examples may use more syncopation, stops and rhythmic contrast.

## 3. Band/style technique notes supplied

### Green Day — Billie Joe Armstrong
- Fast downpicked eighth-note staccato; immediate muting and deliberate right-hand stops create the gaps.
- Verse parts may alternate palm-muted and open attacks. User examples: “Basket Case” and “Longview” as more open; “When I Come Around” as more muted.
- Short melodic solos, often pentatonic / major or Mixolydian-coloured; octave doubling may appear.
- Main riff vocabulary tends to stay in accessible lower-to-middle fretboard positions.
- Downstroke arrows in tabs are a community notation convention, not necessarily present in every transcription.

### Blink-182 — Tom DeLonge
- Octave shapes in open/low positions: root plus octave with the intervening string muted.
- User cites “All the Small Things”, “Dammit” and “Feeling This” as octave-riff references.
- Delayed chord attacks / rests can create a pushed or bouncing groove.
- Verse palm-muting contrasted with open choruses.
- Short melodic phrases, occasional whole-step bends and natural harmonics; abrupt pick attack with little legato apart from connecting hammer-ons.

### Blink-182 — Mark Hoppus (guitar-role reference supplied)
- Simple five-fret pentatonic boxes and lower-position riff vocabulary are suggested.
- Straight eighth-note rhythm with palm muting.
- In layered arrangements, a complementary midrange part may fill space between another guitar part and bass.
- **Verification note:** Mark Hoppus is principally known as the band's bassist. Do not assume these guitar-role claims describe his recorded guitar tracks without track-specific evidence; treat them as a proposed complementary-layer concept pending verification.

### Jerry Finn production / arrangement school
- Double-tracked rhythm guitars, commonly hard-panned left/right; sometimes additional takes.
- User suggests light quantization around 60–70%, retaining human feel while tightening the ensemble. This percentage is an unverified workflow estimate, not a universal production rule.
- Dynamic arrangement: palm-muted verse → open chorus; clean guitar or arpeggios in bridge/breakdown sections.
- Tight metronomic performance with slight forward energy rather than mechanically rigid timing.
- Treat this as a production/arrangement reference, not a mandatory pattern for every song or artist.

### Sum 41 — Deryck Whibley
- More metal-influenced rhythm vocabulary: gallops and 3+3+2 groupings.
- Fast alternate picking for solos; pentatonic and natural-minor vocabulary; tapping and pinch harmonics in some parts.
- “In Too Deep” is supplied as an example of syncopated eighth-note power-chord rhythm.
- Heavier pick attack and more aggressive articulation than the lighter pop-punk examples.

### The Offspring — Noodles
- Palm-muted octave/chug figures and punk-rooted riff patterns.
- Straight downpicked eighth-note parts are common in the supplied description.
- Short solos often use bends and pentatonic phrasing.
- Pre-chorus / chorus transitions may use a full-beat rest or gated stop followed by a strong chord attack.

### New Found Glory
- Hardcore-influenced Drop-D chugs, including repeated low open-string attacks with accents on offbeats.
- Octave riffs in choruses.
- Full open chords after muted passages create a closed/open contrast.

### Simple Plan
- Straight eighth-note downstrokes on power chords; open choruses and comparatively few syncopations.
- Minimal solos; melodic octave hooks may replace conventional solos.
- User proposes this as an accessible foundational rhythm style; this is a teaching comparison, not a product-level difficulty rating.

### Fall Out Boy — Joe Trohman
- Expanded chords and melodic upper notes can replace bare power chords.
- Hammer-ons / pull-offs and tapped introductions are cited as emo-influenced vocabulary.
- Syncopated accents can interact with or oppose the vocal rhythm.
- Guitar hooks and melodic sections may take the role normally occupied by a solo.

### Paramore — Josh Farro
- Syncopated riff accents, including offbeat attacks.
- Drop-D low-string chugs and slides.
- Narrow melodic solo phrases with pentatonic vocabulary and occasional harmonic/glissando accents.

### Avril Lavigne — early albums
- More chord-led strumming, including down/up strokes and accents on weaker beats.

- Verses may use right-hand muting over held chord shapes; riff density is often lower than in heavier pop-punk styles.

### MGK — Tickets to My Downfall (2020)

## 4. Articulation vocabulary supplied

- **Palm mute (PM):** right-hand palm muting near the bridge; an essential controllable performance articulation, often used in verses.
- **Open attack:** release palm muting for a full, sustained chord; key contrast for choruses.
- **Dead note / muted note (x):** percussive string hit without a stable pitched note; distinct from palm-muted pitched notes.
- **Octave voicing:** root and octave with the intervening string muted; characteristic riff texture.
- **Hammer-on / pull-off:** connecting legato notes; more prominent in emo-influenced and some modern styles than in straight classic downpicked riffs.
- **Slide:** connecting notes, sometimes in Drop-D riffs.
- **Bend:** mainly a lead articulation; often a whole-step bend in the supplied examples.
- **Natural harmonics:** especially frets 12, 7 and 5 as accents or phrase endings.
- **Downpicking / alternate picking:** distinct picking-direction behaviours; do not infer all fast eighths must be downpicked.
- **Strummed down/up strokes:** chordal rhythmic articulation distinct from single-note picking.

## 6. Tab-reading and tuning notes supplied

- Picking-direction marks are often omitted from community tabs; do not assume a universal downpicking rule based solely on tempo.
- Power chords may be written on only two or three strings, with other strings intentionally muted.
- Drop-D / Drop-C require tuning-aware chord shapes; in Drop-D, the lowest three strings are not all in standard tuning relationships.
- The user suggests practice starting at 120 BPM with eighth notes, increasing by 10 BPM toward the target tempo. This is a practice recommendation, not a pattern-generation requirement.
- Bass octave reinforcement is a possible arrangement layer, not an automatic rule for every guitar pattern.

## 7. PATTERN-mode and articulation design implications — user confirmed

The user explicitly confirmed that this database is especially suitable for **PATTERN mode** and stylistic **articulation**. Preserve these as design targets for later implementation, within the existing architecture and approved control/state contracts:

1. Patterns represent timed musical events, rests and accents, not merely repeated chord triggers.
2. Preserve picking direction where known: downstroke, upstroke and alternate picking remain distinguishable.
3. Distinguish palm-muted pitched notes, open notes/chords and dead/muted percussive hits. These are not interchangeable states.
4. Retain stylistic rhythmic vocabulary as pattern-building material: straight eighths, gallops, syncopation, 3+3+2 grouping, offbeat accents, gated rests/stops, delayed chord entries, and muted-verse/open-chorus contrast.
5. Preserve tuning and voicing context: Standard, Eb, Drop D and Drop C cannot be treated as interchangeable guitar fingering maps. Pattern playback must resolve intended pitches and voicings correctly.
6. Preserve riff/chord roles: two-/three-string power chords, octave voicings, low-string chugs, expanded chord voicings, strummed chords and single-note melodic hooks.
7. Retain tempo and feel metadata separately from note content where the architecture permits: BPM, meter, subdivision, swing/humanization, accent strength and phrase length. Do not add unapproved user-facing controls solely because these concepts are useful.
8. Preserve arrangement sections and transitions where supported: verse, pre-chorus, chorus, bridge, breakdown, pickup/fill, stop and re-entry.
9. Keep lead articulation vocabulary available for single-note phrases: hammer-on, pull-off, slide, bend and natural harmonic. Only expose or trigger an articulation when the playback engine has a real implementation and suitable source material.
10. Validate every pattern against actual instrument mappings, string/tuning assumptions, MIDI note output and audible playback. A text tab alone is not a playable or READY pattern.
11. PATTERN content should be stylistically reusable and not falsely attributed as an exact artist/song transcription. Use original patterns inspired by broad technique characteristics; avoid copying protected riffs or recordings.

## 8. Preset concept confirmed by the user: one sound identity, two playing contexts

The user clarified that a preset is fundamentally a **guitar sound configuration** and must be useful both in single-note mode and in chord/pattern mode. Apply this as a central design principle during the future preset phase:

- A preset defines the sound identity: source instrument/sample set, tuning/voicing compatibility, pickup/tone character where modelled, velocity response, articulation behaviour, amp/cab or IR, EQ, dynamics and effects.
- The same selected sound should remain coherent when played as isolated MIDI notes, melodic phrases, power chords, octave riffs, strummed chords or PATTERN playback.
- Pattern-specific performance data (rhythm, note sequence, rests, accents and articulation events) is conceptually distinct from the underlying guitar tone. Preserve this distinction in the implementation and file model; do not silently turn every rhythm pattern into a different guitar preset.
- A preset may include style-appropriate default articulation/response mappings when supported, but must not force one pattern or one song's riff on every performance.
- Test every READY sound in at least two contexts: (A) single-note / melody playing and (B) chord / pattern playing. Include muted and open attacks, low and high register, velocity range, note transitions and sustained notes as appropriate.
- A sound that is impressive on isolated notes but collapses, muddies or becomes unnaturally loud in power chords is not READY for this product. Likewise, a rhythm-friendly sound must retain clear note attack and expression for melody.
- Preserve clear separation between RAW/CLEAN/DI/ACOUSTIC source presets and PROCESSED/AMPED/FX sound presets, while ensuring each relevant sound can be auditioned in both playing contexts where technically appropriate.
